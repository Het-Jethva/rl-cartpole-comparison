\begin{abstract}
We compare tabular Q-learning, a deep Q-network (DQN), and REINFORCE with a
learned baseline on CartPole-v1 under a controlled protocol: a single shared
training loop, an identical budget of \StepBudget{} environment steps, a held-out
evaluation set fixed across all methods, an equal hyperparameter search budget per
method on seeds disjoint from those reported, and \NumSeeds{} evaluation seeds per
configuration aggregated by interquartile mean with bootstrap intervals.
Because all three methods solve the task, we do not rank them; we measure what
each one spends to succeed, across sample efficiency, computation, reliability
and implementation cost. The orderings disagree. REINFORCE reaches the solved
threshold first (\ReiSolve{} steps), ends at the maximum return
(\ReiFinal{}), and uses \ReiUpdates{} gradient updates---two orders of magnitude
fewer than DQN's \DqnUpdates{}---with the fewest hyperparameters of the three.
DQN is the most expensive method on every cost axis and leads on none.
Tabular Q-learning is the fastest in wall-clock time and the least reliable: every
seed attains the maximum return at some point, yet its final interquartile mean is
\TabFinal{}, because the discretisation its exact representation requires makes
distinct states indistinguishable. Ablations locate these outcomes precisely:
removing DQN's target network eliminates learning entirely
(\AblNoTargetFinal{}), whereas removing REINFORCE's baseline is costly but
survivable (\AblNoBaselineFinal{}). We conclude that on this task, algorithmic
machinery is worth its cost only in proportion to the difficulty it was designed
to address, and we report all \NumRunsTotal{} runs.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

CartPole \cite{barto1983} is the standard first problem in reinforcement
learning. It is small, it is fast, and every general-purpose method solves it.
That last property is what makes it interesting for a comparison: because the
outcome is not in doubt, the question stops being \emph{which method works} and
becomes \emph{what each method costs to make it work}.

This paper compares three methods that sit at three levels of approximation:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Tabular Q-learning} \cite{watkins1992}, which represents the value
      function exactly over a discretised state space;
\item \textbf{DQN} \cite{mnih2015}, which replaces the table with a neural
      network and adds the two devices---a replay buffer and a target
      network---that make that replacement stable;
\item \textbf{REINFORCE with a learned baseline} \cite{williams1992}, which
      abandons the value function as the object of interest and optimises the
      policy directly.
\end{itemize}

The comparison is not a ranking. All three methods reach a working policy on
CartPole-v1, so a claim of the form ``method X is best'' would be both
uninformative and already known. The contribution here is a \emph{trade-off map}:
a measurement of what each method spends to arrive at that policy, along four
axes that are usually reported separately or not at all---environment samples,
computation, reliability across random seeds, and implementation effort.

Two design commitments make the resulting numbers comparable. First, every method
runs under a single shared training loop with an identical budget of
\StepBudget{} environment steps and an identical evaluation procedure, so no
method can gain an advantage from the harness. Second, hyperparameters are
selected by a random search with an equal trial budget per method, on seeds
disjoint from those used for the reported results. Without the second commitment,
a comparison paper measures how the author distributed their attention.

The contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item A controlled comparison of the three methods on CartPole-v1 over
      \NumSeeds{} seeds each, under a shared budget and a fixed evaluation set.
\item A trade-off table quantifying sample cost, compute cost, reliability and
      implementation cost together, including the implementation-cost axis that
      empirical RL papers normally omit.
\item A sensitivity study for each method over the hyperparameter that carries
      its characteristic failure mode, and ablations of the two components that
      distinguish the deep methods from their textbook forms.
\item A complete, seed-reproducible implementation with the full result set.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background}

\subsection{Problem setting}

Reinforcement learning problems are modelled as a Markov decision process
$(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, r, \gamma)$, where $P(s' \mid s, a)$ is the
transition distribution, $r(s,a)$ the reward, and $\gamma \in [0,1)$ a discount
factor. The agent seeks a policy $\pi(a \mid s)$ maximising the expected
discounted return $\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t} \gamma^{t} r_t\right]$
\cite{sutton2018}.

CartPole-v1 has a four-dimensional continuous observation---cart position, cart
velocity, pole angle and pole angular velocity---and two actions, pushing the
cart left or right. Every timestep yields a reward of $1$. An episode terminates
when the pole angle exceeds $12^{\circ}$ or the cart leaves the track, and is
truncated at 500 steps. The maximum achievable return is therefore 500, and the
conventional definition of solving the task is a mean return of at least
\SolvedThreshold{}.

\subsection{Tabular Q-learning}

Q-learning \cite{watkins1992} estimates the optimal action-value function by the
off-policy update
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
Q(s_t,a_t) \leftarrow{} & Q(s_t,a_t) \\
  & + \alpha \left[ r_t + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s_{t+1},a') - Q(s_t,a_t) \right],
\end{split}
\end{equation}
which converges to $Q^{*}$ under standard conditions when every state--action
pair is visited infinitely often and $Q$ is represented exactly.

That last requirement is the difficulty here. An exact representation needs a
finite state space, and CartPole's is continuous, so the observation must be
discretised into bins. Two of its four dimensions---the two velocities---are
unbounded, so they must additionally be clipped before they can be binned. The
resulting agent therefore does not solve CartPole; it solves a discretised
approximation of CartPole, and the gap between the two is a cost that the method
pays and the other two do not.

\subsection{Deep Q-networks}

DQN \cite{mnih2015} replaces the table with a parameterised function
$Q_\theta(s,a)$ and minimises the temporal-difference error against a bootstrap
target. Two devices stabilise what is otherwise a moving-target regression:

\begin{itemize}
\item a \emph{replay buffer}, which stores past transitions and samples training
      batches uniformly from them, breaking the temporal correlation that would
      otherwise violate the i.i.d.\ assumption of stochastic gradient descent;
\item a \emph{target network} $Q_{\theta^-}$, a periodically refreshed copy of
      the online network used to compute the bootstrap term, which stops the
      regression target from moving with every gradient step.
\end{itemize}

The loss on a sampled transition is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \ell_{\delta}\!\left(
  Q_\theta(s,a) -
  \left[ r + \gamma (1 - d) \max_{a'} Q_{\theta^-}(s',a') \right]
\right),
\end{equation}
where $\ell_{\delta}$ is the Huber loss and $d$ indicates a genuine termination.
Section~\ref{sec:extended} ablates the target network to show what it buys.

\subsection{REINFORCE with a baseline}

REINFORCE \cite{williams1992} optimises a parameterised policy $\pi_\theta$
directly, following the policy gradient
\begin{equation}
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}
\left[ \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\,
\left( G_t - b(s_t) \right) \right],
\end{equation}
where $G_t$ is the Monte-Carlo return from timestep $t$ and $b(s_t)$ is a
baseline. Any function of the state alone leaves the gradient unbiased, so the
baseline is free to reduce variance; the standard choice, adopted here, is a
learned state-value estimate $\hat{V}_\phi(s)$ trained by regression on the
observed returns \cite{sutton2018}.

The baseline is not cosmetic. Because every CartPole reward is $+1$, the raw
return $G_t$ is large and strictly positive, so an unbaselined update increases
the log-probability of \emph{every} action taken in a successful episode,
including the poor ones---the gradient carries the correct signal only through
differences in magnitude. Subtracting $\hat{V}_\phi(s)$ centres the signal so
that only better-than-expected actions are reinforced. This is ablated in
Section~\ref{sec:extended}.

Note that returns are deliberately \emph{not} whitened. Dividing by the batch
standard deviation, common in tutorial implementations, rescales the gradient as
well as centring it; it is an adaptive step size rather than a baseline, and it
would confound the ablation.

\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:design}

The design follows the reproducibility recommendations of Henderson et al.\
\cite{henderson2018} and the statistical recommendations of Agarwal et al.\
\cite{agarwal2021}.

\subsection{Shared budget and a single training loop}

All three agents run under one training loop, differing only in what they do with
a transition. Each run receives exactly \StepBudget{} environment steps, and
learning curves are plotted against environment steps rather than episodes. The
distinction matters: a CartPole episode lasts around 20 steps for an untrained
policy and 500 for a good one, so an episode budget silently grants the better
method up to twenty-five times more data.

The loop bootstraps through a time-limit truncation but not through a genuine
termination. Treating the 500-step truncation as terminal would teach both
value-based methods that balancing the pole indefinitely is worth nothing.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}

Training return is a poor measure of policy quality, because it includes the
cost of exploration and therefore penalises whichever method explores most. Every
\EvalEvery{} steps, training is paused and the agent is evaluated for
\EvalEpisodes{} episodes with exploration disabled: greedy action selection for
the two value-based methods, and the mode of the action distribution for
REINFORCE.

The evaluation episodes use a fixed set of environment seeds, identical for every
method, every seed and every evaluation point. The evaluation set is therefore
held out and constant, so a difference between two curves reflects the policies
rather than a difference in the states they happened to be tested on.

\subsection{Equal-budget hyperparameter search}

Each method receives \NumTrials{} random-search trials drawn from a discrete
space of its own hyperparameters, evaluated on \NumTuningSeeds{} tuning seeds and
scored by area under the evaluation curve---a criterion that rewards reaching a
high return and reaching it early. The winning configuration is then frozen and
run on \NumSeeds{} fresh evaluation seeds. Tuning seeds and evaluation seeds are
disjoint, so no reported result is tuned on its own test set. The full search
spaces and the selected values appear in Appendix~A.

\subsection{Statistics}

Reinforcement learning results across seeds are skewed and frequently bimodal: a
run either solves the task or collapses. The sample mean describes neither mode,
and one failed seed in ten moves it substantially. Following Agarwal et al.\
\cite{agarwal2021}, the point estimate is the \emph{interquartile mean} (IQM),
the mean of the middle 50\% of the seeds, and uncertainty is reported as a 95\%
percentile bootstrap interval resampled over seeds. Per-seed final performance is
also plotted directly, so that any bimodality remains visible rather than being
absorbed into an aggregate.

\section{Implementation}

The implementation is a single Python package. All three agents expose the same
four-operation interface---select an action, accept a transition, finish an
episode, report diagnostics---so the training loop, the evaluation procedure and
the logging are written once and shared. Every behavioural difference between the
methods lives in the update.

Both neural methods use a two-layer multilayer perceptron with 128 hidden units
per layer and ReLU activations, optimised with Adam. DQN uses this network for
$Q_\theta$ and a periodically synchronised copy for $Q_{\theta^-}$; REINFORCE
uses one for the policy logits and a second, separately optimised network for the
baseline $\hat{V}_\phi$.

One configuration object fully determines one run, and a hash of it names that
run's output files. This makes the run matrix resume-safe---an interrupted sweep
restarts without repeating completed work---and it means every curve in this
paper can be traced to the exact settings that produced it. Runs execute in a
process pool, one run per worker, each pinned to a single thread so that the
reported wall-clock times are not distorted by workers competing for cores.

Correctness is protected at the points where an error would be silent rather than
loud. A wrong sign in the advantage or an off-by-one in the discounted return
does not raise an exception; it produces a plausible learning curve and a wrong
paper. The discounted-return computation, the state discretiser's clipping
behaviour, the replay buffer's contents under overwrite, and the aggregation
statistics are therefore checked against hand-computed values, and every method
is checked to reproduce its curve exactly from a fixed seed.

Every figure and table in this paper is generated from the raw result files, and
every number quoted in the text is a macro produced by the same stage, so the
prose cannot drift out of step with the data.

\subsection{Sample efficiency}

REINFORCE with a baseline reaches the solved threshold first, at a median of
\ReiSolve{} environment steps, against \DqnSolve{} for DQN. This is the reverse
of the usual expectation---replay-based value methods are normally the
sample-efficient choice, because each transition is reused many times---and it is
a consequence of the task rather than of the algorithms. CartPole's reward is
dense and its episodes are short, so a Monte-Carlo return is a low-variance
learning signal available almost immediately, and the bias-free policy gradient
has little to overcome. DQN spends its early budget filling a replay buffer and
waiting for a bootstrapped value estimate to become self-consistent.

The tabular agent is the more interesting case. It first touches the threshold at
a median of \TabSolve{} steps---\emph{earlier than DQN}---but this is not evidence
that it learns a better policy. It is evidence that the threshold is a transient
event for this method rather than a stable state, which the next subsection makes
explicit.

\subsection{Final performance and reliability}

At the end of the budget, REINFORCE attains an interquartile mean of \ReiFinal{}
(95\% CI [\ReiCiLow{}, \ReiCiHigh{}]), that is, the maximum return the environment
permits, on every seed in the interquartile range. DQN attains \DqnFinal{}
[\DqnCiLow{}, \DqnCiHigh{}]. The tabular agent attains \TabFinal{} [\TabCiLow{},
\TabCiHigh{}]---less than half.

Fig.~\ref{fig:scatter} shows why the aggregate alone would be misleading. The
tabular agent's per-seed final returns are spread across almost the whole
available range, despite every seed having reached a return of 500 at some earlier
point in training. The agent does not converge to a mediocre policy; it oscillates
between good and poor ones, and the final measurement catches it at an arbitrary
point in that oscillation. This is the expected behaviour of Q-learning under
function approximation coarse enough to violate its convergence conditions: with
the selected discretisation of \TabCells{} cells, distinct physical states share a
table entry, so no fixed point of the tabular update corresponds to a good policy
in the underlying continuous problem.

Supporting this, only \TabCoverage{}\% of the table's cells are ever visited
within the budget, even though the search selected the \emph{coarsest} of its
candidate discretisations. Finer grids were available and were rejected: a finer
grid represents the dynamics better but cannot be filled with \StepBudget{}
samples. That tension---representational fidelity against sample cost---is the
tabular method's characteristic trade-off, and Section~\ref{sec:extended}
measures it directly.

DQN reaches the threshold on all \NumSeeds{} seeds, but one seed ends the budget
far below its own peak, which is what pulls its final interquartile mean below
REINFORCE's. This is the familiar instability of bootstrapped value learning:
performance is not monotonic, and a policy that has been solved can be unlearned.

\subsection{Computational cost}

The compute ordering is different again, and it separates into two distinct
quantities that the wall-clock column alone would conflate.

Measured in gradient updates, REINFORCE is cheaper than DQN by more than two
orders of magnitude: \ReiUpdates{} against \DqnUpdates{}. DQN performs one
minibatch update per environment step by construction, whereas REINFORCE performs
one update per completed episode---and as its policy improves, its episodes
lengthen to 500 steps, so it updates \emph{less} often the better it gets. Its
compute cost per unit of experience falls during training, which is unusual and
worth noting.

Measured in wall-clock seconds the tabular agent is cheapest at \TabTime{}\,s,
against \ReiTime{}\,s and \DqnTime{}\,s, because a table update is a single
arithmetic operation with no gradient and no framework overhead. It performs the
most updates of any method---one per step---and is still by far the fastest.

\subsection{Implementation cost}

The final column of Table~\ref{tab:tradeoff} is the axis empirical comparisons
usually omit. Counting executable source lines in the agent module and the number
of tunable hyperparameters, the tabular agent costs \TabLoc{} lines and
\TabHyper{} hyperparameters, REINFORCE \ReiLoc{} lines and \ReiHyper{}, and DQN
\DqnLoc{} lines and \DqnHyper{}.

DQN is the most expensive method to implement and to configure, on a task where
it finishes second in sample efficiency and second in final return. Its replay
buffer, its target network and the six hyperparameters that govern them exist to
solve problems---correlated samples, moving regression targets, high-variance
returns over long horizons---that CartPole does not pose severely enough to
justify the cost.

\section{Extended Analysis}
\label{sec:extended}

This section reports work beyond the originally proposed scope: a sensitivity
sweep for each method over the hyperparameter that carries its characteristic
failure mode, and an ablation of each stabilising component in the two deep
methods. Sweeps use \NumSeedsSweep{} seeds per setting; ablations reuse the full
\NumSeeds{} evaluation seeds.

\subsection{Sensitivity}

Fig.~\ref{fig:sweeps} shows that the three methods are fragile along entirely
different axes, and to very different degrees.

\textbf{Tabular Q-learning is sensitive to discretisation, and not monotonically
so.} Final interquartile means range from \TabSweepWorstValue{} at the coarsest
grid (324 cells) to \TabSweepBestValue{} at the finest (82{,}944 cells), a spread
of \TabSweepSpread{} return. The relationship is not, however, ordered: the
1{,}024-cell grid selected by the search reaches 253 with no failed seeds, while
the intermediate 5{,}184- and 16{,}384-cell grids reach only 153 and 170, and the
16{,}384-cell grid fails on 60\% of its seeds.

Two competing effects produce this shape. A finer grid represents the dynamics
more faithfully, which raises the ceiling; but it also spreads a fixed sample
budget over more cells, which slows learning and leaves most of the table
unvisited. The coarsest grid is bad for the first reason and the intermediate
grids are bad for the second. The finest grid recovers because CartPole's start
states are tightly clustered, so a fine grid concentrated on the small region the
agent actually visits behaves like a much smaller table---at the cost of a 40\%
seed failure rate. The search's choice was not the highest-scoring setting but the
most reliable one, which is the correct selection under an area-under-curve
criterion.

\textbf{DQN is sensitive to target-network staleness, with an interior optimum.}
The spread is the largest of the three at \DqnSweepSpread{} return. Synchronising
the target network every update destroys learning outright (final return
\DqnSweepWorstValue{}, no seed ever solving the task), because it removes the
separation between prediction and target that makes the regression well posed.
Synchronising every 500 updates is best (\DqnSweepBestValue{}, no failed seeds).
Intervals that are too long are also harmful but less catastrophically: at 1{,}000
and 5{,}000 updates the agent still learns, from a target that is increasingly out
of date, and fails on 40\% of seeds.

\textbf{REINFORCE is the least sensitive of the three.} Across two orders of
magnitude of learning rate the final return varies by only \ReiSweepSpread{}, and
four of the five settings end above the solved threshold. What the learning rate
changes is speed and reliability rather than eventual quality: $10^{-4}$ solves at
50k steps, $10^{-3}$ at 6k, and $3\times10^{-3}$ at 4k but with a lower final
return, while $10^{-2}$ reaches a high final return yet fails on 40\% of seeds.

This last panel also exposes a limitation of the search rather than of the method.
The selected learning rate of $3\times10^{-4}$ solves at 20k steps, whereas
$10^{-3}$---also in the search space---solves at 6k with an identical final
return. With \NumTrials{} trials over a joint space of four hyperparameters, the
search sampled a good configuration rather than the best available one. The
reported REINFORCE result is therefore, if anything, conservative.

\subsection{Ablations}

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{figures/ablations.pdf}
\caption{Component ablations against the full method. Removing DQN's target
network eliminates learning entirely; removing REINFORCE's baseline slows

\label{fig:ablations}

Fig.~\ref{fig:ablations} and Table~\ref{tab:ablations} isolate what each
stabilising component contributes.

\textbf{The target network is not an optimisation for DQN; it is a precondition.}
Removing it drops the final interquartile mean from \DqnFinal{} to
\AblNoTargetFinal{}---barely above the return of a random policy---and no seed
reaches the threshold at any point in training. Without it, the network's own
prediction supplies the regression target, so improving the prediction moves the
target, and the two chase each other instead of converging.

This result also serves as an internal consistency check. The ablation and the
$\text{interval}=1$ arm of the DQN sweep implement the same thing through
different code paths---one disables the target network, the other refreshes it
every update---and they agree to within a point of final return
(\AblNoTargetFinal{} against \DqnSweepWorstValue{}). Agreement between two
independent routes to the same condition is evidence that neither is an artefact.

\textbf{The baseline is a substantial improvement for REINFORCE, but not a
precondition.} Removing it lowers the final interquartile mean from \ReiFinal{}
to \AblNoBaselineFinal{}, delays the median solve from \ReiSolve{} to
\AblNoBaselineSolve{} steps---more than double---and raises the seed failure rate
from \ReiFailure{}\% to \AblNoBaselineFailure{}\%. The confidence interval widens
dramatically, which is the signature the theory predicts: the baseline is a
variance-reduction device, so removing it should degrade consistency more than it
degrades the best case, and Fig.~\ref{fig:ablations} shows exactly that shape---a
band that spans nearly the full range of possible returns.

The asymmetry between the two ablations is itself informative. DQN's target
network addresses a structural defect that makes the objective ill-posed, so its
removal is fatal. REINFORCE's baseline addresses variance in an objective that is
already well-posed and unbiased, so its removal is expensive but survivable.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{There is no single winner, but there is a pattern}

Across the four axes measured, no method leads on all of them. The tabular agent
is the cheapest in wall-clock time and among the cheapest to implement, and it is
by a wide margin the least reliable. DQN is the most expensive method on every
cost axis---implementation, configuration, gradient updates, wall-clock---and it
does not lead on any performance axis. REINFORCE with a baseline reaches the
threshold soonest, ends highest, and uses the fewest updates and the fewest
hyperparameters, but it is a Monte-Carlo method whose advantages here rest on
properties of the task rather than on generality.

The pattern is that method complexity should be matched to task difficulty rather
than assumed to dominate it. Each of DQN's components addresses a specific
failure: the replay buffer addresses correlated samples, the target network
addresses a moving regression target, and both are indispensable on the
high-dimensional, long-horizon problems the method was designed for
\cite{mnih2015}. CartPole poses neither problem severely. The machinery is
therefore paid for and largely unused, which is exactly what the trade-off table
shows.

\subsection{Why the expected ordering reverses}

Textbook intuition predicts that a replay-based value method is more sample
efficient than a Monte-Carlo policy gradient, because replay reuses each
transition many times while REINFORCE discards each episode after one update.
That intuition assumes the Monte-Carlo return is a noisy signal. On CartPole it
is not: the reward is dense, every episode terminates within 500 steps, and the
return is therefore a bounded, low-variance estimate available at the end of every
episode. With a learned baseline removing most of the remaining variance, the
unbiased policy gradient simply has little to gain from bootstrapping---and it
avoids bootstrapping's cost, which is a value estimate that must become
self-consistent before it is useful.

The result should therefore be read as a statement about the interaction between
a method and a task, not as a ranking of the methods. It predicts that the
ordering would change under a sparse reward or a long horizon, where the
Monte-Carlo return becomes high-variance and bootstrapping earns its cost.

\subsection{Discretisation is a modelling decision, not a preprocessing step}

The tabular results make the clearest single point in this study. Q-learning is
guaranteed to converge to the optimal action-value function under an exact
representation \cite{watkins1992}. Applying it to CartPole requires abandoning
that condition, and the consequence is not a slightly worse policy---it is the
loss of a stable one. The agent reaches maximum return on every seed and then
fails to stay there, because the discretisation makes states that require
different actions indistinguishable.

Choosing the bin count is therefore not a tuning detail on the same footing as a
learning rate. It defines what problem is being solved. The search selected the
coarsest available grid, which is the correct choice under a
\StepBudget{}\,step budget and simultaneously the choice that guarantees the
representation is inadequate. There is no setting that escapes both horns within
this budget, which is precisely the argument for function approximation.

\subsection{Practical reading}

For a practitioner facing a low-dimensional, densely rewarded control problem, the
evidence here favours starting with a policy gradient and a learned baseline: it
was the simplest of the three to implement, needed the fewest hyperparameters,
required the least tuning effort to work, and performed best. Reaching for a
replay-based value method should follow evidence that the task demands it---long
horizons, sparse reward, or expensive environment interaction that makes sample
reuse worth its overhead---rather than preceding that evidence.

\section{Threats to Validity}

\textbf{A single environment.} Every result here comes from CartPole-v1. The task
is low-dimensional, densely rewarded, and solvable by a linear policy, and it is
therefore the most favourable possible setting for the tabular method: four
dimensions is few enough to discretise at all. On a task with a higher-dimensional
observation, the table would grow exponentially while the two neural methods would
be unchanged in size. The direction of that effect is predictable, but its
magnitude is not measured here, and no result in this paper should be read as a
claim about reinforcement learning in general.

\textbf{Dense reward.} CartPole gives a reward at every timestep, which suits
Monte-Carlo policy gradients particularly well. Under a sparse reward, REINFORCE
receives an informative gradient only from the rare successful episode, and the
comparison could reverse. Testing that would need a second environment.

\textbf{Search budget.} The hyperparameter search is equal across methods by
construction, but it is small---\NumTrials{} trials over a discrete grid. A larger
budget could favour whichever method has the most delicate configuration, most
plausibly DQN, which has twice as many hyperparameters as REINFORCE. The equality
of the budget is defensible; its size is a limitation.

\textbf{Ten seeds.} \NumSeeds{} seeds is enough for an interquartile mean to be
meaningful and enough to expose a bimodal outcome, but it is too few for
well-powered significance testing between arms whose intervals overlap. No
pairwise significance claim is made anywhere in this paper for that reason.

\textbf{Implementation cost is partly subjective.} Source lines and hyperparameter
count are objective once the counting rule is fixed, but the rule involves
judgement: the shared training loop is excluded, and a differently factored
implementation would produce different numbers. The column is best read as an
ordering, not as a measurement.

\textbf{Wall-clock time is machine-specific.} All runs executed on the same
machine, one thread each, so the comparison between methods is fair; the absolute
values are not portable, and the ratio would change on a GPU---where the two
neural methods would gain and the tabular method would not.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper compared tabular Q-learning, DQN, and REINFORCE with a learned
baseline on CartPole-v1 under a shared budget of \StepBudget{} environment steps,
a fixed held-out evaluation protocol, an equal hyperparameter search budget per
method, and \NumSeeds{} evaluation seeds per configuration, reporting
interquartile means with bootstrap intervals over \NumRunsTotal{} runs in total.

The comparison does not produce a ranking, because the ordering of the three
methods changes with the cost being measured. Tabular Q-learning is the cheapest
to run and the least reliable: it reaches maximum return on every seed and holds
it on none, because the discretisation its exact representation demands destroys
the distinctions the task requires. DQN is the most expensive method to implement,
to configure and to compute, and on this task it leads on no performance axis;
its stabilising machinery addresses difficulties CartPole does not present.
REINFORCE with a baseline reaches the threshold soonest, ends at the maximum
return, and does so with the fewest hyperparameters and two orders of magnitude
fewer gradient updates than DQN.

The ablations locate these outcomes in specific components rather than in the
methods as wholes, and the sensitivity sweeps show that each method's
characteristic fragility lies on a different axis: representational granularity
for the tabular agent, target-network staleness for DQN, and step size for
REINFORCE.

The central limitation is the single environment. CartPole's low dimensionality
is what makes a table feasible at all, and its dense reward is what makes
Monte-Carlo returns cheap; both conditions favour the methods that performed best
here. The natural next step is to repeat this protocol on a task that breaks one
condition at a time---a higher-dimensional observation to stress the table, and a
sparse reward to stress the Monte-Carlo return---which would test whether the
trade-off structure reported here is a property of the methods or of CartPole.
